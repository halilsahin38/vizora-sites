# 4. When your story is messy {.chapter}

Chapter 3 works beautifully when your situation is clean: big layoff, whole team, nothing complicated. Real life is often messier. Maybe only you and one other person were let go. Maybe it wasn't really a layoff. Maybe you quit before they could push you. Maybe this is your second layoff in two years.

This chapter goes through the messy versions one by one. The principle stays the same in every case: **be honest about the fact, be brief, and put the weight of your answer on what you learned or what you're doing now.**

## "Only a few of us were let go"

This is the situation that makes people most nervous, because it invites the question *why you?*

Don't wait for that question. Answer it inside your main answer, using whatever the real criteria were. Companies usually cut based on something: which projects survived, which locations stayed open, seniority, cost of the role, or a team being merged.

> "The company reduced my team from six to three. They kept the people working on the product line they were continuing, and I'd been on the one they decided to close. My manager is one of my references, and she's happy to talk about my work."

That last sentence is gold if it's true. Offering a reference from the manager who was there when you left tells the interviewer more than any explanation could. If you parted on good terms with your manager, ask them now whether they'll be a reference. Most people say yes.

If you honestly don't know why you were chosen, don't guess and don't speculate. Say what you do know.

> "They didn't share the individual criteria, but it was part of a wider cost-cutting round. My reviews in the two years before were strong, and I'm happy to share them."

## "Honestly, I was let go for performance"

If you were fired, or "managed out," don't call it a layoff. This feels tempting, but it's the one thing in this book I'd ask you never to do. A reference check or a single follow-up question can expose it, and then the interviewer stops trusting anything else you said.

You can be honest without making it the headline. The structure is: own it briefly, explain the mismatch, show what you've done about it.

> "Honestly, the role wasn't the right fit. It was much more focused on cold sales than I'd understood when I joined, and after eight months we agreed it wasn't working. It taught me a lot about what I'm good at. I'm strongest when I'm managing existing customers and growing those accounts, which is why I'm applying for account management roles like this one."

Notice that this answer doesn't grovel and doesn't blame. It treats the firing as useful information about fit. That's a mature way to see it, and interviewers respect it.

If the issue was something you've genuinely worked on, say so with specifics. "I've since done a course in Excel modelling and built three financial models for a friend's business" is far more convincing than "I've worked on it."

## "I quit before I had another job"

Plenty of people leave without a plan. Burnout, a toxic manager, a move, a family situation, or simply seeing the layoffs coming and getting out first. The worry is that it sounds impulsive.

The key word is *decision*. Frame it as a choice you made deliberately, not something that happened to you.

> "I decided to leave in the summer. The company was going through a lot of cuts and I wanted to make my next move on my own terms rather than wait. It's given me the time to be selective, and this is one of only a handful of roles I've applied for."

Don't say "I quit because my manager was terrible," even if they were. You can be honest about the general situation ("the team had a lot of turnover and it wasn't an environment where I could grow") without naming villains.

## "I left because of burnout or health"

You don't owe anyone details about your health, mental or physical. You only need to answer the employer's real question, which is *are you ready to work now?*

> "I took some time off to deal with a personal matter. That's fully resolved, and I'm rested and really keen to get back to it."

If they push for details, which a good interviewer won't, you can say: "It was a health matter, and it's behind me. I'd rather focus on what I can bring to this role." That's a complete, polite answer.

## "This is my second (or third) layoff"

Two layoffs in a row can feel like a pattern, and you might worry the interviewer will think *this person is always the one cut*. Usually, it's the industry. Tech, media, retail and startups all go through waves of cuts that hit good people.

Name the pattern before they do, and then point to what's consistent about *you*.

> "I've worked in media for most of my career, and both of my last two employers went through big cuts. In both roles, though, I was the one who [built the newsletter from zero / ran the biggest client / trained new hires]. Part of what attracts me here is that you've been growing steadily for years, and I'd love to be somewhere I can build something over the long term."

## "I'm still there, but my job is ending"

This is actually a strong position, and you should present it as one.

> "My role is being phased out at the end of next month as part of a restructuring. I'm finishing the handover of my accounts, and I wanted to start looking early so I can make a thoughtful move instead of a rushed one."

## "I left a long time ago and did something else in between"

If you were laid off years ago and then freelanced, cared for someone, studied or did odd jobs, the layoff barely matters anymore. Lead with what you did most recently and only mention the layoff if asked. We'll go deeper into this in the next chapter.

# 5. The gap on your résumé {.chapter}

The longer the job search goes, the more a second worry creeps in: the gap. Every week without a job feels like another mark against you. By month four or five, people start dreading the dates on their résumé almost as much as the layoff itself.

Here's the honest picture. **A gap is mostly a problem if you look uncomfortable about it.** Interviewers want to know two things: what you did with the time, and whether you're ready now.

## How long is "long"?

There's no official rule, but this is roughly how a gap tends to be read.

| Gap | How it's usually seen | How much to say |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 3 months | A normal job search. Often nobody even asks. | One sentence, only if asked. |
| 3 to 6 months | Very common, especially in a slow market. | Reason plus one thing you did. |
| 6 to 12 months | They want to see you kept your skills fresh. | Reason, activity, and why you're ready. |
| Over 12 months | They want reassurance you're committed and current. | Bring it up yourself, with concrete activities. |

## The three-sentence gap answer

You'll recognise the shape from Chapter 3.

**Why there's a gap.** One sentence. "After the layoff, I took a few weeks off and then started a focused search."

**What you did with it.** One or two sentences. This is where you show you didn't just sit still.

**Why you're ready now.** One sentence pointing at this job.

> "After the layoff in May, I took a couple of weeks to reset and then started searching properly. I've been selective, and I've also used the time to get my Google Analytics certification, which I'd wanted to do for a while. So I feel more up to date than when I left, and I'm ready to commit to the right team."

## What counts as "doing something"

People often think they have nothing to show for their gap, when actually they do. Here's what counts, and how to say it.

- **A course or certificate**, even a free one. "I completed a course in project management to fill a gap I'd noticed in my last role."
- **Freelance, contract or cash-in-hand work.** "I did a few freelance bookkeeping jobs for small local businesses." If it was for friends or family, that counts too, as long as it was real work.
- **Volunteering.** "I've been helping a local food bank with their scheduling and stock system." This shows character *and* skills.
- **Caring for someone.** "I was looking after my mother after her surgery. She's doing well now, and I'm fully available." You don't need to say more than that.
- **A personal project.** Building a website, writing, renovating, organising an event. If you can link it to a skill the job needs, mention it.
- **The search itself.** If you've genuinely been searching hard, you can say so. "I've been selective, because I want the next role to be one I stay in" is a perfectly respectable answer.

## If you really have nothing to show yet

Start something today. I mean that literally. Sign up for a free online course in your field this evening. Offer to help a friend's small business with something you're good at. Write three LinkedIn posts about what you learned in your last job.

In two weeks you'll be able to say "I'm currently doing X," and it'll be true. That's all it takes. Nobody expects a gap to be a period of heroic productivity. They just want to see that you're moving.

## The gap answer for career changers

If you used the gap to switch fields, the gap becomes part of your story rather than a hole in it.

> "When my role in retail management ended, I decided to move into HR rather than go back to the same thing. I spent the last few months doing an HR fundamentals course and helping a friend's company set up their onboarding process. Ten years of managing store teams gave me a lot of practical people experience, which I think is an advantage in an HR role."

# 6. "So, tell me about yourself" {.chapter}

It's usually the first question. It sounds easy and it isn't. And for people who've been laid off, it hides a trap: **they start with the layoff.**

"Well, I was at Company X for six years until I was laid off in March, so I'm looking for…"

You've just made the least interesting thing about you the first thing they hear. The interviewer hasn't even asked about it yet, and now it's framing everything else you say.

Don't do that. The layoff belongs near the end of this answer, in one sentence, or not at all. If they want to know why you left, they'll ask, and you have your thirty-second answer ready.

## Present, past, future

The simplest structure for "tell me about yourself" is three steps.

**Present.** Who you are, professionally, in a sentence. What you do and what you're good at.

**Past.** One or two specific things you've done that prove it. Numbers help.

**Future.** Why this job, now.

Aim for about a minute. Under 45 seconds feels thin. Over two minutes feels like a speech.

## An example

> "I'm a customer success manager. I've spent about six years working with mid-sized business clients in software, and what I'm best at is taking unhappy accounts and turning them into long-term customers. At my last company I looked after about forty accounts, and over a year we brought our churn down from 14% to 8%, mostly by starting regular check-ins nobody had been doing before. That role ended when the company cut its European team in the summer. Since then I've been focusing on roles where retention is a real priority, and from what I've read about your plans for next year, it's a big one here."

The layoff is there. It's one sentence. It's sandwiched between an achievement and a reason for wanting this job. That's exactly where it belongs.

## What to leave out

- Your whole career history, year by year. They have your résumé.
- Where you grew up, your hobbies, your family (unless it connects directly to the job).
- Anything negative about any employer.
- Weaknesses, doubts, or "I'm not sure if I'm the right fit, but…"
- The details of the layoff.

## If you're changing careers

Lead with the *skill* that carries over, not the old job title.

Instead of: "I've been a teacher for eight years, but I'm trying to get into corporate training."

Try: "I design and deliver training. For the last eight years I've done that as a secondary school teacher, planning lessons for 150 students a year and training new teachers in our department. I've realised the part I enjoy most is the training design itself, so I've been moving into corporate learning, and I recently built a full onboarding course for a local business as a project."

Same person. One version sounds like someone running away from something. The other sounds like someone running towards something.
