# 1. The part nobody talks about {.chapter}

Most interview advice starts with techniques. I want to start one step earlier, because the techniques don't work if this part is still raw.

When people get laid off, they usually go through some version of the same few feelings. Not in a neat order, and not once. They come back, especially the night before an interview.

**Shock.** Even when everyone saw it coming, it's strange when it actually happens. One day you have a badge and a login and a calendar full of meetings. The next day you don't.

**Anger.** At the company, at a specific manager, at the people who kept their jobs, sometimes at yourself for not leaving earlier.

**Shame.** This one is the sneakiest. You know, logically, that layoffs are business decisions. But some part of you still wonders what people think. You avoid telling old colleagues. You change the subject at family dinners. You feel like there's a mark on you.

**Doubt.** *If I was really good, would they have let me go?* That question can grow very loud after a few weeks of rejections.

Here's why this matters for your interview. **Whatever you haven't dealt with comes out in your voice.**

Anger comes out as a little edge when you mention your old employer. Shame comes out as over-explaining, apologising, or rushing past the topic so fast that it sounds like you're hiding something. Doubt comes out as hedging. "I think I was pretty good at… I mean, I tried to…"

Interviewers don't need to know what you're feeling to notice that something is off. They just notice that the answer doesn't sit right. And when an answer doesn't sit right, people fill in the blanks themselves, usually with something worse than the truth.

## You don't have to be "over it"

I'm not going to ask you to be at peace with what happened. That can take months. You only need to get to a place where you can talk about it **calmly for thirty seconds**. That's a much smaller goal, and you can reach it on purpose.

Three things help.

**Write the angry version first.** Before you write your interview answer, take ten minutes and write down everything you'd say if there were no consequences. What they did wrong. What was unfair. Who you blame. Don't hold back. Then close the notebook or delete the file. You're not going to use any of it. The point is to get it out of your system so it doesn't sneak into the version you *do* use.

**Separate the decision from your value.** Write down three things you did in your last job that you're genuinely proud of. Specific things: a project, a customer you turned around, a process you fixed, a person you trained. Those things happened. The layoff doesn't undo them. You'll need these later anyway, so you might as well write them now.

**Say it out loud to someone safe.** A friend, a partner, your mum, your dog. "I was laid off in March when the company cut the operations team." Say it until it stops feeling like a confession and starts feeling like a fact. Because that's what it is.

::: {.example}
**Sara** worked in marketing at a software company for five years. When her team was cut, she was furious, mostly because she'd just launched a campaign that beat its targets. In her first two interviews, she spent so long explaining the campaign and how the layoff "made no sense" that she ran out of time to talk about what she wanted next. She didn't get either job.

Before her third interview, she did the angry-version exercise. Two full pages. Then she wrote one sentence: *"My whole team was cut in a restructuring in January, a few weeks after I'd launched a campaign that beat its lead target by 30%."* Same facts. No edge. She got a second interview.
:::

Nothing about Sara's situation changed between interview two and interview three. Only how she carried it.

# 2. What the interviewer is actually checking {.chapter}

When an interviewer asks why you left your last job, it feels personal. It feels like they're poking at a bruise. But from their side of the table, it's a fairly routine question, and they're listening for three specific things.

You can think of them as three silent questions behind the spoken one.

## Silent question 1: "Was this about your performance?"

This is the big one. A layoff because the company cut costs is a non-issue for most employers. Being let go because you couldn't do the job is a different story. The interviewer wants to know which one it was, and they usually can't ask directly without sounding rude.

So they listen for clues. Was it a group of people or just you? Is there a business reason? Does the candidate mention anything they achieved? Does the candidate sound confident or defensive?

**Your job is to answer this question before they have to ask it.** A short phrase like "the whole team was cut" or "my position was eliminated when they closed that division" does most of the work. A concrete achievement does the rest.

## Silent question 2: "Are you honest with me?"

Interviewers have heard every version of every story. They're not trying to catch you out, but they are paying attention to whether your answer sounds straight. Vague answers make people suspicious. So do answers that sound rehearsed in a slippery way, or that change slightly when they ask a follow-up.

Plain, specific facts sound honest. "In March, the company cut about 15% of staff. My team of eight was reduced to three, and I was one of the five who left." That's clear, it's checkable, and there's nothing to dig into.

## Silent question 3: "Are you going to bring that baggage here?"

Nobody wants to hire someone who's still fighting their last job. If you criticise your old company, the interviewer isn't thinking "how awful for you." They're thinking "what will this person say about *us* in two years?"

They also want to know you're looking forward. Are you excited about this role, or just relieved to be offered anything?

**So the best answers are short, factual, and end on the future.** That's not a coincidence. It's the shape that answers all three silent questions at once.

## What they mostly *don't* care about

It helps to know what you can stop worrying about.

They generally don't care about the inside politics of your old company. They don't need the timeline of who said what in which meeting. They don't need to hear how unfair it was, even if it was. They don't need your severance details or your feelings about your old manager.

They do care about your skills, your results, how you work with people, and whether you want *this* job.

So the layoff should take up as little of the interview as possible. Not because it's shameful, but because it's not the interesting part of you.

# 3. Your 30-second answer {.chapter}

This is the heart of the book. If you only take one thing from it, take this chapter.

Your answer to "Why did you leave?" or "What happened at your last job?" should have three parts and take about thirty seconds. Some people call this kind of structure a formula. I'd rather you think of it as a path you walk down every time: **what happened, why it wasn't about you, and where you're going now.**

## Part 1: What happened

One sentence. Plain facts. No warm-up.

The mistake almost everyone makes is starting with a run-up. "So, well, it's a bit of a long story, um, basically there were some changes at the company…" By the time you get to the actual fact, the interviewer has already decided something went wrong.

Just say it.

> "I was part of a layoff in April when the company cut its customer support team."

> "My role was eliminated after we were acquired by a larger company that already had someone doing the same job."

> "The startup I worked for ran out of funding in February and let most of the team go."

That's it. It feels too blunt when you write it down. It doesn't sound blunt when you say it calmly. It sounds confident.

## Part 2: Why it wasn't about you

One sentence that answers silent question number one. The best way to do this isn't to *say* you were good. It's to *show* it with something specific.

Weak: "I was a really good employee and I always worked hard."

Strong: "In my last year there I brought our average ticket resolution time down from two days to under one."

Weak: "It wasn't a performance thing."

Strong: "The whole department went, including our director."

You only need one of these. Either a fact about the scale of the layoff, or a fact about your results. If you have both, pick the result. Results are more interesting.

## Part 3: Where you're going now

One or two sentences that turn the conversation towards this job. This is the part people forget, and it's the part that makes the biggest difference. It answers silent question three. It tells them you're not stuck in the past.

> "Since then I've been looking specifically for a support role at a growing company, because I enjoyed building processes more than anything else, and that's a big part of this job."

> "It actually gave me the push to focus on logistics planning, which is what I was best at, and that's why this role jumped out at me."

Then stop talking.

This is hard. When you're nervous, silence feels dangerous, so you keep going. You add a detail, then a justification, then an apology. Don't. Say your three parts, smile, and let them ask the next question. A calm pause after a clear answer is one of the most confident things you can do in an interview.

## Putting it together

Here's a full answer, spoken at a normal pace. Time it if you like. It's about 25 seconds.

> "I was part of a layoff in April, when the company cut its customer support team from twelve people to four. In my last year there I'd brought our average resolution time down from two days to under one, so I'm proud of what I left behind. Since then I've been looking for a role where I can build support processes at a company that's growing, which is exactly what drew me to this one."

Notice what's not in there. No blame. No apology. No "unfortunately." No story about the director. No explanation of why they chose four people to keep. Nothing for the interviewer to dig into, because there's nothing hidden.

## Rewriting a bad answer

Let's look at the kind of answer people give when they haven't prepared, and fix it step by step.

::: {.example}
**The unprepared answer:**

"Yeah, so, that's a good question. Basically the company was going through a lot of changes. We got a new CEO about a year ago and he had a very different vision, and honestly a lot of us didn't really agree with the direction. Then in March they announced restructuring, and they did it pretty badly to be honest, it was just a 15-minute call. I'd been there six years so it was a bit of a shock. I think they were mainly trying to cut costs before selling the company. But anyway, I'm looking for something new now."
:::

What's wrong with it? It opens with a stall. It talks about disagreeing with leadership (warning sign). It criticises how the layoff was handled (more baggage). It mentions the shock (emotion). It speculates about the company's motives (not your business anymore). And it ends with "I'm looking for something new," which says nothing about *this* job.

Now the rewrite:

::: {.example}
**The prepared answer:**

"After six years there, my role was one of about forty cut in a restructuring in March. In that time I'd grown from coordinator to managing the regional supplier accounts, and I renegotiated contracts that saved around €200,000 a year. What I want next is a bigger procurement role at a company that's investing in its supply chain, and from what I've read about your expansion, that's what's happening here."
:::

Same person. Same facts. Completely different impression.

## How to make it sound like you

The examples in this book are deliberately neutral. If you copy them word for word, they'll sound a bit like a script, because they are one. Here's how to make yours sound natural:

- **Use your own words for your job.** If you'd say "I ran the warehouse night shift," don't say "I was responsible for overseeing nocturnal logistics operations."
- **Keep one detail that's specific to you.** A number, a customer type, a tool you used. Specific details make you sound real.
- **Write it down, then say it, then rewrite it the way you said it.** Spoken language is shorter and looser than written language. Your answer should sound like speech.
- **Practise it five times out loud.** Not in your head. Out loud. The first time will feel awkward. By the fifth, it'll feel like something you'd actually say.
