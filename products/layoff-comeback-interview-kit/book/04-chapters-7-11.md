# 7. Getting your confidence back before you walk in {.chapter}

You can have perfect answers on paper and still walk into the room feeling small. A few weeks of silence from employers will do that to anyone. So before we go further into questions and answers, let's work on the person who's going to be giving them.

Confidence before an interview isn't a personality trait. It's mostly preparation plus evidence. You can build both.

## Build a "proof file"

Open a document or grab a notebook and write down everything you've achieved at work. Not your job duties. Things that happened *because you were there*.

Some questions to get you going:

- What problem did you fix that had been bothering people for a while?
- When did a customer, colleague or manager thank you for something specific?
- What numbers improved while you were responsible for them? Sales, time, costs, errors, complaints, sign-ups.
- Who did you train or help get better?
- What would have gone worse if you hadn't been there?

Write at least ten. It'll feel hard at first and then it'll start flowing. These become the raw material for your stories (Chapter 8) and your "tell me about yourself" answer. But they also do something else: they remind you, in black and white, that you were good at your job. Read the list the morning of your interview.

## Prepare your stories, not just your answers

Most interviews include "tell me about a time when…" questions. A time you handled conflict. A time you failed. A time you worked under pressure. People panic on these because they try to come up with a story on the spot.

You don't have to. If you prepare **six or seven good stories** from your proof file, you can adapt them to almost any question. One story about a big achievement might answer "your proudest moment," "a time you went above and beyond" and "a time you showed initiative."

For each story, jot down four things:

- **The situation.** Where were you, what was going on? One or two sentences.
- **Your task.** What were you responsible for?
- **What you did.** This is the biggest part. Use "I," not "we." What exactly did *you* do?
- **The result.** What changed? A number is best. A clear outcome is fine.

That structure is often called STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result), and interviewers know it well. Spoken out loud, each story should take about a minute and a half.

There are story worksheets in the Toolkit.

## Record yourself (yes, really)

This is the exercise everyone skips and the one that helps most.

Put your phone on the table. Hit record. Ask yourself the three big questions out loud: "Tell me about yourself," "Why did you leave your last job?" and one "tell me about a time" question. Answer each one as if the interviewer were sitting across from you.

Then listen back once. Just once. You're listening for three things:

- **Length.** Was the layoff answer under 30 seconds? Was "tell me about yourself" under two minutes?
- **Filler.** Did you start with "so, um, basically"?
- **Tone.** Did your voice change when you talked about your old job?

Fix one thing, record again. Most people need three or four rounds before it sounds natural. It's uncomfortable to hear yourself. It's much more uncomfortable to hear yourself ramble in the actual interview.

## Look after the basics

This sounds obvious, but after weeks of job searching, a lot of people are running on bad sleep, irregular meals and too much screen time. You think more clearly when you've slept. You sound calmer when you've eaten. You have more energy when you've been outside that day. None of it is complicated, and all of it shows up in an interview.

# 8. The follow-up questions that trip people up {.chapter}

You've given your thirty-second answer. It went well. Then they ask a follow-up, and that's where unprepared people come undone. Here are the follow-ups that come up most for laid-off candidates, what's really being asked, and how to answer.

## "Why do you think they chose you?"

*What they're really asking:* Was it performance?

Don't guess and don't get defensive. Give the real criteria if you know them, and offer evidence.

> "It was based on which projects the company kept. Mine was in the area they closed. My last two reviews were strong, and my former manager is happy to be a reference."

## "Were you a low performer?"

Rarely asked this bluntly, but it happens.

> "No, and I'm happy for you to check. In my last year I [specific result]. The decision was about the budget for our department, not individual performance."

Keep your voice level. The question isn't an accusation. It's a box they need to tick.

## "How do you feel about your old company?"

*What they're really asking:* Will you be negative about us one day too?

> "Honestly, mostly grateful. I learned a lot there, especially about [skill]. Nobody enjoys a layoff, but I understand why they did it."

Even if you don't feel grateful yet, there's probably *something* you learned there. Lead with that.

## "What have you been doing since you left?"

You've already prepared for this in Chapter 5. Give your activities, briefly.

> "Three things, mainly. A focused job search, a course in [subject] that I'd been meaning to take, and some freelance work for a couple of small businesses, which kept me sharp."

## "Why do you want this job?"

*What they're really asking:* Do you actually want *this* job, or just *a* job?

This is where research pays off. Say something specific about the role or the company that you couldn't say about any other.

> "Two things stood out. The role is mostly about improving the onboarding process, which is the part of my last job I enjoyed most. And you're expanding into Germany next year, and I've managed German-speaking customers before."

## "Why should we hire you?"

Match your strongest proof to their biggest need.

> "From the job description and what you've told me today, your biggest challenge is [their problem]. I've solved that before: at my last company I [specific result]. And I'm available now, so I could start making a difference quickly."

## "Tell me about a time you failed."

Don't use the layoff as your failure. It wasn't your failure, and it pulls the conversation back to the topic you want to keep short. Pick a real work mistake, show how you handled it, and what you do differently now.

> "Early in my last role, I underestimated how long a data migration would take, and we missed the first deadline by two weeks. I told my manager as soon as I realised, re-planned it with the team, and we delivered the rest on time. Since then I always build in a buffer and flag risks in the first week, not the last."

## "Tell me about a conflict."

Choose a story where you disagreed with someone, handled it like an adult, and the outcome was good. Avoid stories where the other person was simply a villain.

> "A colleague in finance and I disagreed about how to report returns. Instead of escalating, I asked her for half an hour to understand her side. It turned out the auditors needed it a certain way, which I hadn't known. We agreed on a format that worked for both teams, and it became the standard."

## "What's your biggest weakness?"

Choose something real but not central to the job, and show what you're doing about it.

> "I tend to take too much on myself instead of delegating. On a big project last year that slowed us down, so I started breaking tasks out at the start and assigning them in our first meeting. I'm much better at it now, though I still have to remind myself."

## "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

*What they're really asking:* Will you stay, or leave the moment something better comes up?

> "Growing here, ideally. Getting really good at [area] and taking on more responsibility, maybe training newer people. After the last year, I'm looking for somewhere I can build something long-term."

## "Do you have other interviews going on?"

Be honest, and turn it into interest.

If yes: "I'm talking to a couple of other companies, but this one is my first choice because [reason]."

If no: "I've been quite selective, and this is one of the few roles I've really gone after."

# 9. How not to sound desperate (even if you are) {.chapter}

Let's be honest. If you've been out of work for a few months, you might *be* a bit desperate. The savings are going down. You're doing maths at the kitchen table. Every interview feels like it has to work.

That's real, and there's nothing wrong with feeling it. The problem is when it leaks into the interview, because desperation makes interviewers nervous. It suggests you'd take any job, which suggests you might leave as soon as a better one comes along. It also tends to make people undersell themselves.

Here's how it usually shows up, and what to do instead.

## The words

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| "I really need this job." | "I'm really interested in this role." |
| "I'll take anything." | "I've been selective, and this one stood out because…" |
| "I'm flexible on everything." | "I'm flexible on [specific thing], and what matters most to me is [thing]." |
| "Sorry, I'm a bit nervous." | Just take a breath. Nobody needs the commentary. |
| "I know I've been out of work for a while, but…" | Don't bring it up. If they ask, use your gap answer. |
| "I was just doing my job." | "I [action] and it [result]." |

## Salary

This is where desperation costs you actual money. The instinct is to name a low number so you don't price yourself out. Resist it.

Do your homework before the interview. Look up the typical salary range for this role in your area on job sites, salary comparison sites, or by looking at similar job ads. Then, if they ask, give a range based on the market, not on your fear.

> "Based on what I've seen for similar roles in this area, I'm looking at roughly [range]. I'm open to discussing it depending on the whole package."

If your last job paid more and this one pays less, and you're fine with that, say why.

> "I know it's a bit below what I was earning before. I'm comfortable with that because [the role is what I want / the stability matters more to me now / there's room to grow here]."

## The energy

Desperation often looks like overcompensating. Talking too fast, agreeing with everything, laughing too hard, saying yes before they've finished the question. Slow down. Pause before you answer. It's fine to say "That's a good question, let me think for a second." Calm reads as competent.

## Remember you're choosing too

The best mindset shift I know is this: **you're interviewing them as well.** You're about to spend forty hours a week there. You deserve to know if it's a good place to work. Prepare real questions (there's a list in the Toolkit) and ask them as if the answers matter to you. Because they do.

# 10. Interview day {.chapter}

You've done the thinking. Now it's about not letting small practical things trip you up.

## The evening before

- Read the job description one more time and highlight the three things they seem to care about most.
- Read your notes on the company. Know what they do, roughly how big they are, and one recent piece of news.
- Say your three key answers out loud once each: "tell me about yourself," your layoff answer, your gap answer if you have one.
- Choose three questions to ask them.
- Lay out your clothes. Aim for one step more formal than what people at the company wear day to day.
- Check the route and leave room for delays. For a video call, test the link.
- Go to bed at a normal time. Last-minute cramming makes you tired, not prepared.

## The morning of

- Eat something, even if you're not hungry.
- Read your proof file. Just the list. Let it remind you who you are.
- Arrive about ten minutes early. Not thirty. Sitting in reception for half an hour does nobody's nerves any good.

## In the room

- Greet everyone, use their names, and make eye contact.
- When you get a question, it's fine to pause. A two-second pause looks thoughtful.
- Keep your layoff answer short and then stop talking.
- If you go blank, say so lightly: "Sorry, let me start that again." It happens to everyone and almost nobody minds.
- Take a notebook. Writing down a key point while they talk shows you're listening, and it gives you something to do with your hands.

## Video interviews

Video interviews are now completely normal, and they have their own small traps.

- Put the camera at eye level. A stack of books under a laptop works fine.
- Sit facing a window or a lamp, not with light behind you.
- Clear the background or use a plain wall. Avoid fake backgrounds if they glitch around your head.
- Close every other app and turn off notifications.
- Look at the camera when you're talking, not at your own face on screen.
- Have your phone's hotspot ready in case your internet drops.
- A sticky note with three keywords next to the camera is fine. A full script on screen is not. People can see your eyes reading.

## Recorded video interviews

Some companies send a link where you record answers to questions on your own, with no person on the other end. They feel strange, but the same rules apply. Use the practice question if there is one. Keep answers to about a minute or a minute and a half. Smile at the start of each answer. A real person will watch it later, even if it doesn't feel that way.

# 11. After the interview {.chapter}

You walk out, and your brain immediately starts replaying everything you said wrong. That's normal. Let it run for ten minutes, then do something useful with it.

## Write down what happened

While it's fresh, note down which questions surprised you, which answers went well, and which you'd change. Every interview is practice for the next one. People who keep these notes get noticeably better within three or four interviews.

## Send a thank-you email the same day

It's a small thing that a surprising number of candidates skip. Keep it short, mention one specific thing from the conversation, and restate why you're a good fit. There's a template in the Toolkit.

## While you wait

Don't stop applying because one interview went well. This is the most common mistake people make, and it can cost weeks. Keep your search going until you've signed an offer.

If they gave you a date and it passes with no word, wait two or three working days and send a polite follow-up. One follow-up is professional. Five is not.

## If you get a "no"

It's going to happen. It happens to everyone, including people who eventually land great jobs. A rejection usually means someone else fit a bit better on that day, not that something is wrong with you.

Reply to the rejection. Thank them, say you enjoyed meeting them, and ask if they have any feedback. Most won't give much, but some will, and occasionally a company will come back months later when another role opens. You'd be surprised how often the person who replied graciously is remembered.

Then go back to your notes, adjust one thing, and get ready for the next one.

# A last word {.chapter}

When you've been laid off, it's easy to feel that this one event is the whole story of your working life right now. It isn't. It's one sentence in a much longer story, and you get to decide how much space that sentence takes up.

In your next interview, say it calmly. Say it in thirty seconds. Then spend the rest of the time talking about what you've done, what you're good at, and what you want to do next. That's the part of the story that gets people hired.

You've done hard things at work before. You've got a list of them now. Go and add another one.

Good luck. You've got this.
